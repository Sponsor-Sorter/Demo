# Meadow Market Littles: next play experiences

Research and design brief, 21 October 2026. These ten proposals are now playable in Littles Build 16 as a first single-player pass. Story scenes, farm arrangements, market selections and keepsake stickers save locally on the device. This pass does not yet include linked versions; the existing twelve mini-games continue to cover listening, memory, matching, sorting, counting, patterns, animal homes, picture puzzles and comparing quantities.

## What the market signals — and what it does not

The US Apple App Store Kids free chart currently places Toca Boca World, Lingokids, LEGO Builder, ABCmouse, colouring, Bluey, reading, PBS KIDS and Sago Mini among its visible leaders. This is a point-in-time chart, not a download or play-time dataset. Google Play describes Toca Boca World as an open-ended, touch-first, child-paced world for stories, homes, shops and role play, and reports 100M+ installs. Those examples suggest strong interest in creative worlds, role play, building, colouring and guided learning. They do not show that Littles should copy any one game's size, progression, monetisation or retention tactics.

The product opportunity is to make Meadow Market feel like a familiar place children can return to and change, while keeping the existing small, complete games easy to find. New activities should create reasons to play another kind of play, not add pressure to keep a streak or beat a clock.

## Design rules for every new experience

- Aim the first version at ages 3–5, then add optional choices for 5–6 year olds. A child should be able to understand the next action from a short spoken cue and a clear picture, without needing to read.
- Make the whole play scene a single full-screen composition. Keep every button, target, instruction and modal inside that composition on phones and tablets, in portrait and landscape. The scene art is the canvas; UI never floats beyond it.
- Use large, forgiving touch targets, tap-to-place as well as drag where it suits the activity, visible selection, undo, and gentle recovery when something lands in the wrong place.
- Use existing farmer, animal, produce and outcome recordings where they fit. Replay is always available. Add new spoken lines only where current clips cannot teach the action. Keep all activities usable when sound is muted.
- Keep child-created arrangements on the device by default. No public sharing, chat, microphone recording, competitive leaderboard, forced account or purchase prompt in a child activity.
- Give children a satisfying ending or a natural stopping point. Replays and collections should invite choice without streaks, countdown pressure, daily-loss mechanics or manipulative reward loops.
- Build for one child first. A linked mode can be added only when the same activity is understandable and fair with two children taking turns or cooperating on one shared scene.

## Proposed experiences

### 1. Make a Meadow Story

**Play promise:** “Put the farm friends where you like and make up what happens.”

Start with one calm full-screen scene such as the meadow, barnyard or market. A tray holds three familiar characters and a small set of props. Children tap a character to hear its name, then drag or tap it into the scene. Props can be moved, swapped or removed at any time. Characters respond with a short existing sound or gentle animation when they meet an object. There is no required plot and no fail state.

For a guided option, a farmer offers one pictorial prompt: “Who needs a snack?”, “Where should the lamb rest?” or “Can the apple visit the market?” Accept several sensible placements and let the child continue the story afterward. Save one local scene snapshot and let the child name it from a small set of picture symbols; do not require typing.

**Growth:** Add another scene and a few more props as optional choices. Do not gate the basic story set behind scores.
**Linked play:** Two children arrange the same scene in turns, with a clear “your turn” token and no remote chat.
**Why it is next:** The existing games have answers and rounds. This gives imaginative, child-led play without competing with the drawing studio.

### 2. Build a Little Farm

**Play promise:** “Choose a place for the barn, pond, garden and windmill.”

Present a broad landscape and a tray of large, distinctive farm pieces. Tap-to-place and drag both work. Pieces snap to a gentle grid but can overlap where it makes sense. A single undo button reverses the last move; a reset asks for an adult-style confirmation only after the child taps it. The completed farm can be visited by the existing characters and saved locally.

Offer an open canvas and a “help Farmer Jade” card with a picture goal, such as “make room for the ducks by the pond.” The card is a suggestion, not a test. Buildings and paths must remain readable on a small phone screen, with the play area and tray fitted inside the full-screen background.

**Growth:** Unlock additional decorative pieces through play variety, never repeated grinding. Keep the original pieces available.
**Linked play:** Shared building, alternating one placement at a time; either player can undo their own last placement before the next turn.
**Why it is next:** Building is visible in popular kids’ experiences and makes the farm itself a persistent toy.

### 3. Find the Hiding Farm Friend

**Play promise:** “Can you spot the hen behind the hay?”

Show a quiet scene with one named or pictured friend hidden in plain sight. A farmer names the target and offers one optional visual clue. Tapping a decoy gives a warm hint, not a penalty. The target gives a cheerful reveal animation. Start with a single large, partially visible friend and a simple scene; later use more visual clutter only in an optional older-child setting.

**Growth:** Rotate scene, target animal, and clue type (colour, location, sound). Never hide the target behind UI or require pixel-precise tapping.
**Linked play:** One child gives the clue by choosing a picture card; the other finds the friend, then swap.
**Why it is next:** The current “What Changed?” asks children to compare two images. This is a different, single-scene observation game that supports picture vocabulary.

### 4. From Seed to Snack

**Play promise:** “Help the seed grow, then pick something for the basket.”

Use a short, tactile sequence: choose a seed, pat the soil, add water, watch a simple growth animation, then collect the crop. Every step uses one large action and a clear visual change. A child can replay a step or ask the farmer to show it again. Avoid implying that every plant grows at the same speed or in the same way; use a small set of clear examples.

**Growth:** Introduce a few crop families and a simple order-the-pictures recap for children who want a puzzle. The recap is optional; harvesting remains the main reward.
**Linked play:** One child plants and waters, the other picks and packs; roles switch for the next crop.
**Why it is next:** It turns existing crop art, audio and market activities into a connected cause-and-effect story.

### 5. Dress for the Farm Weather

**Play promise:** “The day is rainy. What can keep the lamb cosy?”

Show one friendly character and a clear weather picture. Offer two or three clothing or shelter items, each with a large icon and spoken name. Children dress the character or prepare its shelter. Several choices can be accepted if they make sense; the farmer explains the match with one short sentence. Keep this focused on weather words and caring, not body judgement or fashion scoring.

**Growth:** Add sun, wind and cool-day scenes, with a small wardrobe that can be mixed freely after the guided choice.
**Linked play:** One player chooses weather; the other prepares the character, then reverse roles.
**Why it is next:** This adds gentle pretend play and practical vocabulary beyond the current fixed sorting rules.

### 6. Little Market Pretend Play

**Play promise:** “Let’s fill the customer’s basket together.”

Create a tiny role-play stall. A customer arrives with a picture order and optional spoken request. The child chooses produce, puts it in a basket, and hands it over. The customer responds with a brief thank-you and an expressive animation. No prices, checkout pressure, scarcity, or real-money concepts. Children can also open free play and invent their own basket.

**Growth:** Vary the customer, order size and stall layout. Offer a one-item order by default and let older children choose a larger picture list.
**Linked play:** One child is the customer and selects a picture order; the other fills the basket. Swap after delivery.
**Why it is next:** It joins market sorting and spoken-order mechanics into a more social, make-believe loop.

### 7. Farm Shape Shadow Match

**Play promise:** “Which shape fits the windmill window?”

Present one large silhouette cutout and two or three chunky shape pieces. Children tap a piece, then tap its outline; use forgiving placement and a visible fit preview. Start with circles, squares and triangles in high contrast. Name the shape aloud when selected. Keep shape recognition distinct from the existing four-piece scene jigsaw.

**Growth:** Combine two shapes into a simple object, then let children decorate it in open play. Do not introduce timed matching.
**Linked play:** One child chooses a piece and the other places it.
**Why it is next:** It adds early geometry with reusable farm art and a different spatial task from picture-piece reconstruction.

### 8. Make a Barnyard Sound Garden

**Play promise:** “Choose who plays: the hen, the cow, or the wind chimes.”

Let children tap large character and object buttons to trigger familiar animal sounds or gentle instrument sounds already available. Arrange up to three sounds in a row, then play the little sequence back. A solo free-play mode has no wrong sounds. A guided mode asks children to copy a short sound pattern, complementing the existing picture-based spoken pattern game.

**Growth:** Offer simple slow/fast feel and start/stop controls, not a tempo score. Keep simultaneous sound count modest and include a clear stop button.
**Linked play:** Each child adds one sound to the same shared sequence.
**Why it is next:** It extends Farm Band and sound recognition into child-directed composition rather than another quiz.

### 9. Follow the Tractor Trail

**Play promise:** “Help the tractor reach the apple tree.”

On a simple farm map, children place one or two picture-direction tiles (forward, turn) and tap “go.” The tractor moves along a visible path. Begin with a single straight route and a small number of choices; later add one gentle turn. The child can clear the route and try again without losing anything.

**Growth:** Optional route challenges add a bridge or pond to go around. Keep routes short, visible and deterministic. No text code or abstract syntax.
**Linked play:** One child places the next direction; the other starts the tractor and checks the route.
**Why it is next:** It introduces simple sequencing and spatial planning with a familiar farm vehicle.

### 10. Meadow Day Keepsakes

**Play promise:** “Look what you made and did on the farm.”

Create a private, local-only scrapbook of a few child-selected moments: a farm picture they coloured, a tiny farm they built, a story scene they arranged or a crop they grew. Children can decorate a page with stickers and choose a weather or animal stamp. Nothing is uploaded or publicly shared. An adult can clear saved pages from settings; children can revisit or delete their own page with a simple confirmation.

**Growth:** Pages are created by choosing to save, not automatically or as a daily attendance reward. Keep the library small and make storage usage visible to the adult.
**Linked play:** Children can each add one sticker to a shared local page, if both devices are already connected.
**Why it is next:** It creates continuity between separate games and gives children a reason to revisit their own creations without a streak or competitive score.

## Suggested build order

1. **Make a Meadow Story** — validates child-led play and reuses current art/audio.
2. **Build a Little Farm** — creates persistent play and extends scene composition.
3. **Little Market Pretend Play** — connects existing sorting and spoken order in an expressive role-play loop.
4. **Find the Hiding Farm Friend** — low-asset, short-session observation game.
5. **From Seed to Snack** — adds gentle causal progression and crop animation.
6. **Dress for the Farm Weather** — brings care and vocabulary into play.
7. **Farm Shape Shadow Match** — adds geometric reasoning.
8. **Make a Barnyard Sound Garden** — expands music play with a calmer compose mode.
9. **Follow the Tractor Trail** — adds early sequencing once the main scene builder is stable.
10. **Meadow Day Keepsakes** — build only after local-save size, parent controls and privacy behavior have been reviewed.

## Release gate for each activity

Do not list a proposal as a finished game until it has a complete play loop, replay and exit controls, audio replay/mute support, a sensible quiet mode, responsive full-screen layout with all controls inside the scene art, a safe way back to the catalogue, and a checked asset cache entry. Keep solo play complete. Add a linked variant only after its turn/state behavior fits the existing local pairing connection without disconnecting players between games.
